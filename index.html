Why Wrong

“You got it wrong” is the least useful sentence in education.

A red X tells a student nothing and a teacher less. Every wrong answer on this test is mapped to a specific, named misconception — so instead of a score, you get the mental model that produced it, the handful of failure modes your class actually shares, and an audit of which of your questions measured anything.

Method

Four stages. Two of them are not a language model.

  1. Distractor mapping

    Every wrong option on the test is tagged with the misconception that produces it. This is how real concept inventories are built — the Force Concept Inventory in physics is the canonical example — and it is what makes a wrong answer informative rather than merely negative.

  2. Per-student inference no LLM

    A misconception is latent: you never observe it, only answers it makes more likely. A student who holds one still gets the item right sometimes, and a student who does not still slips onto that option occasionally. So evidence is accumulated across every probing item as a log-odds update — naive Bayes over item responses — rather than read off a single wrong answer.

  3. Class structure no LLM

    Each student becomes a point in 12-dimensional misconception space. Students with no confident misconception are separated out first — they are not a failure mode, they are the students who are fine. The rest are clustered by direction rather than magnitude, so a student who expresses the sign misconceptions weakly still groups with those who express them strongly. k is chosen by silhouette, with ties broken toward fewer groups, because a teacher can act on three groups and not on eleven.

  4. Prescription

    Each failure mode gets a reteach aimed at the specific belief, plus verification items to check it landed. Because those verification items exist, the loop closes: reteach, re-run, watch the cluster shrink.
