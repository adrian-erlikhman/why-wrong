How it works

Four steps, from a multiple-choice quiz to knowing the reteach worked.

  1. 1

    Give a quiz where every wrong answer means something

    Each wrong option is written to catch one specific misunderstanding, the way research tests such as the Force Concept Inventory are built. Algebra 1 and Evolution come built in. For any other topic, AI writes the questions and the app checks them before they are used.

  2. 2

    Paste back the class’s answers

    One row per student, copied straight out of a spreadsheet or a Google Forms export. Print the quiz, the answer key and a blank answer sheet from Materials. Everything runs in the browser, so student data never leaves the page, and you can save the class to a file on your own computer.

  3. 3

    See who believes what, and what to reteach

    It weighs every answer to estimate which misunderstandings each student holds, groups the students who share one, gives each group a reteach and three check questions, and flags the questions on the test that measured nothing. The diagnosis comes from statistics, not AI, and every student’s result shows the evidence behind it.

  4. 4

    Give it again and see who moved

    After the reteach, the same paper compares every student with themselves. You see which group’s lesson landed, who needs it again, and whether the change is bigger than chance.

    Does any of this work?

    A fair question, and the sample class exists to answer it. Every simulated student was given a hidden set of misunderstandings before any answers existed, and their answers were generated from it. The analysis never sees that hidden set.     Four stages. Two of them are not a language model.

    The division of labour is deliberate. A model writes the questions, because knowing how students go wrong in a subject is the kind of work it does well. Every judgement about your students is arithmetic, because arithmetic is reliable and can be checked. You can check it yourself on any student and any question.

    1. Questions built to diagnose model-authored

      On an ordinary test the wrong answers are filler. Here every wrong option is tied to one specific misunderstanding, so choosing it means something. This is how concept inventories are built (the Force Concept Inventory in physics is the standard example), and it is the reason a wrong answer can carry information instead of just costing a mark.

      Built-in subjects are hand-written. New ones are generated, then checked against the same structural rules (enough questions per misunderstanding, every wrong option mapped, exactly one right answer) and repaired until they pass. Nothing runs on a test that failed the check.

    2. Working out what each student believes no model

      You never observe a misunderstanding directly, only answers that make it more likely. A student who holds one still gets the question right sometimes; a student who doesn’t still slips onto that option occasionally. So no single question can diagnose anybody, and evidence is accumulated across every question that tests the same point.

      Because the arithmetic is a sum of one term per question, it comes apart again the same way. That is exactly what the evidence trail on each student is: not an explanation written afterwards, but the actual sum that produced the number.

      A blank is not a wrong answer. It adds nothing to the sum, so a skipped question neither counts against a student nor clears them. Anyone who answered under half the paper is listed as having too few answers, because no evidence is not the same as good news.

    3. Finding the groups no model

      Students who are wrong in the same way get grouped together, by what they believe rather than by score, so two students with very different grades can land in the same group. Students with no stable misunderstanding are set aside first, because they don't need a reteach.

      How many groups is decided by the data, with a floor on group size. That floor is a teaching constraint rather than a statistical one: a group of two is not something you can plan a lesson around, it is two students.

    4. What to do on Monday model-adapted

      Each group gets a reteach aimed at the specific belief, plus three questions to check it landed. Then give the paper again and After the reteach compares every student with themselves, and says whether the change is bigger than chance. Diagnoses near the line flip between sittings with no teaching at all, so it looks at how many students moved each way rather than at the counts, using an exact McNemar test.

    Build questions for any topic

    Name a topic and a model will write the misunderstandings students have in it, plus a question bank where every wrong option is tied to one of them. The result is checked against the same structural rules as the built-in subjects, and repaired if it falls short, before any analysis runs.
