Does any of this actually work?

A fair question, and the sample class exists to answer it. Every simulated student was given a hidden set of misunderstandings before any answers existed, and their answers were generated from it. The analysis never sees that hidden set. Below is how much of it came back, computed live on the class you are looking at.

Four stages. Two of them are not a language model.

The division of labour is deliberate. A model writes the questions, because knowing how students go wrong in a subject is knowledge-intensive work it is genuinely good at. Every judgement about your students is arithmetic, because arithmetic is reliable and can be checked — and you can check it, on any student, on any question.

  1. Questions built to diagnose model-authored

    On an ordinary test the wrong answers are filler. Here every wrong option is tied to one specific misunderstanding, so choosing it means something. This is how genuine concept inventories are built — the Force Concept Inventory in physics is the standard example — and it is the reason a wrong answer can carry information rather than just costing a mark.

    Built-in subjects are hand-written. New ones are generated, then checked against the same structural contract — enough questions per misunderstanding, every wrong option mapped, exactly one right answer — and repaired until they pass. Nothing runs on an instrument that failed the check.

  2. Working out what each student believes no model

    You never observe a misunderstanding directly, only answers that make it more likely. A student who holds one still gets the question right sometimes; a student who doesn’t still slips onto that option occasionally. So no single question can diagnose anybody, and evidence is accumulated across every question that tests the same point.

    Because the arithmetic is a sum of one term per question, it comes apart again the same way. That is exactly what the evidence trail on each student is: not an explanation written afterwards, but the actual sum that produced the number.

  3. Finding the groups no model

    Students who are wrong in the same way get grouped together, by what they believe rather than by score — so two students with very different grades can land in the same group, which is the whole point. Students with no stable misunderstanding are set aside first, because they are not a failure mode, they are the students who are fine.

    How many groups is decided by the data, with a floor on group size. That floor is a teaching constraint rather than a statistical one: a group of two is not something you can plan a lesson around, it is two students.

  4. What to do on Monday model-adapted

    Each group gets a reteach aimed at the specific belief, plus three questions to check it landed. Because those questions exist, the loop closes: reteach, re-run, watch the group shrink.

Build questions for any topic

Name a topic and a model will write the misunderstandings students actually have in it, plus a question bank where every wrong option is tied to one of them. The result is checked against the same structural rules as the built-in subjects, and repaired if it falls short, before any analysis runs.

Paste your class

One row per student, one column per question. Copy straight out of a spreadsheet. Answers can be letters (A B C D), numbers (1 2 3 4), or the answer text itself — letters and numbers as printed on the paper from Materials. Leave a cell blank if a student skipped it — blanks count as no evidence rather than as a wrong answer.
