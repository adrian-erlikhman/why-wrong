A score shows which answers were right or wrong. Why Wrong looks for patterns that may point to misunderstandings, groups students with similar evidence, and flags questions worth reviewing.

How it works

Four steps, from a multiple-choice quiz to checking what changed after a reteach.

  1. 1

    Start with questions designed to reveal misunderstandings

    Some wrong options are designed to point to a specific misunderstanding; others may be slips or guesses. Algebra 1 and Evolution & Inheritance are included. For another topic, an AI model can draft questions, which you should review before using with a class.

  2. 2

    Add your class’s answers

    One row per student, copied straight out of a spreadsheet or a Google Forms export. Print the quiz, the answer key and a blank answer sheet from Materials. Everything runs in the browser. Class answers stay on your device, and you can save the class to a file on your computer.

  3. 3

    Review patterns and plan what to revisit

    The analysis combines answers to estimate which misunderstandings may be present. It groups students with similar evidence, suggests a reteach and three check questions for each group, and flags questions worth reviewing. It also notices when the same students keep choosing wrong answers your questions don’t label, which may be a misunderstanding nobody wrote down. The analysis uses statistics, not AI, and each result shows the evidence behind it.

  4. 4

    Compare a retest

    After the reteach, give the same paper again and compare each student’s results. You can see which patterns changed and whether the change is unlikely to be chance. Since students have seen the paper before, treat the result as evidence, not proof.

    How well does the method recover known patterns?

    The sample class lets us compare the analysis with known, simulated answers. Each student starts with a hidden set of misunderstandings; the analysis does not see it. These measures show how much of that hidden information the analysis recovers. They are a simulation check, not a guarantee of results in a real class.

    What AI does — and what it doesn’t.

    An AI model can help draft a question set for a new subject. The analysis of student answers uses statistics, not AI, and you can inspect the evidence for each result.

    1. Questions designed to reveal patterns model-authored

      Some wrong options are linked to a specific misunderstanding; others are treated as slips because they do not provide a clear signal. This gives the analysis a starting point for finding patterns across a student’s answers.

      Built-in subjects are hand-written. New question sets can be generated with an AI model, then checked for structural problems such as missing answer choices or unmapped options. These checks do not confirm subject accuracy, so review generated questions and labels before giving them to students.

    2. Estimating what each student may understand no model

      A response cannot reveal a student’s thinking with certainty. Students may answer correctly despite a misunderstanding, or choose a distractor by mistake. The analysis combines evidence across questions that test the same idea and estimates how strongly the answers support each possible misunderstanding.

      Three things a real class does are built in. Each student’s own accuracy is estimated from their answers, so a student who guesses a lot is not labelled with a misunderstanding every time a guess lands on its option. Wrong options that tempt everyone are recognised from how the whole class answered, and so is whether the options written for a misunderstanding catch careless errors across the whole paper: each question has only a few slips to learn from, the paper has hundreds. On simulated classes where those options tempt everyone, that roughly halved false labels on the weakest students. And a question that tests two misunderstandings a student holds can only show one of them, so picking one is not counted against the other.

      The evidence trail shows which answers raised or lowered each estimate. It is the same calculation used to produce the result, not a separate explanation written afterwards.

      A blank is not a wrong answer. It adds nothing to the sum, so a skipped question neither counts against a student nor clears them. Students who answered fewer than half the questions are marked as having too few answers. Missing evidence is not evidence that everything is fine.

      A particular misunderstanding is reported only when the student answered at least two questions designed to test it. If fewer were answered, that pattern stays untested and does not drive a diagnosis or reteach group.

      It also needs at least two of those answers to point to it. Tested on simulated classes built to break the method’s assumptions, an estimate resting on a single answer was right less than half the time, so those are shown as worth one more question instead. So are estimates just under the line that two answers point to, which were right a little more than half the time. Neither changes a diagnosis or a group; each comes with the question that would settle it.

    3. Finding the groups no model

      Students with similar answer patterns are grouped together, regardless of score. Students without a clear pattern are kept out of the reteach groups; that does not prove they have no misunderstandings, only that this test did not identify a stable one. Those of them who are still getting most questions wrong are named separately: scattered errors that frequent point to a gap or guessing, which needs practice and a check-in.

      The data helps choose how many groups to show, subject to a minimum group size. The minimum reflects a practical limit: very small groups are harder to plan a lesson around.

      Some students sit almost as close to a second group as to their own. On the same simulated classes, about half of them had been put in the wrong group, against roughly one in eight of everyone else. So each group card names them under “Check where they fit”, with the other group, instead of presenting their placement as settled.

      A question set cannot anticipate every misunderstanding. When students share one it does not label, the same students keep picking the same unlabelled wrong answers. The analysis looks for those, comparing each question only among students who picked an unlabelled answer on it, so weaker students and the labelled misunderstandings cannot produce the pattern by themselves. The whole search is then repeated on 100 copies of the class with those answers shuffled at random, and a pattern is shown only if it is larger than 95 of them produce. “Does it work?” tests this live by hiding one of the sample class’s misunderstandings from the analysis. If you can tell what links the answers, name it on the card: the test gets it as a new misunderstanding and the analysis tracks it from then on, in a copy of a built-in test or in your own.

    4. Plan a reteach and check for change model-adapted

      Each group gets a reteach suggestion and three check questions. Give the same paper again to compare results for each student. The comparison accounts for changes in both directions and estimates whether the overall shift could be due to chance. Because students have seen the questions before, treat the result as evidence rather than proof.
